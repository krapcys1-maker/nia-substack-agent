# Art direction — the visual identity of NIA's covers
---
Copy this treatment into every brief, word for word, after your scene
sentence. It is the owner's own block and it is not yours to reword:

> Sophisticated mid-century editorial magazine illustration, hand-painted
> gouache and ink appearance, crisp angular fashion illustration, restrained
> Art Deco geometry, cinematic composition, subtle screen-print texture,
> visible paper grain and slightly imperfect vintage registration.
>
> Palette: warm ivory, charcoal black, deep forest green, muted olive,
> brushed steel and brass, with burnt orange and vermilion used only as
> strong narrative accents.
>
> Elegant, intelligent, darkly humorous and faintly unsettling. Polished but
> not glossy. No photorealism, no cartoon robots, no neon cyberpunk, no text,
> no logos, no watermark.

## Where these scenes take place

Grand mid-century interiors, never a modern office. Marble and terrazzo in
black and cream, dark green wall panels, fluted columns, strict geometric
partitions. Brass and steel machinery with its gears showing. Tall plants in
stone planters. A burnt-orange disc on the wall whenever it earns its place.

People wear tailored 1950s and 60s clothing: sharp suits, hats, coats over
the arm, court shoes. Uniformed staff where the uniform makes the joke.

The covers belong in the grand lobby, the examination hall, the bank vault,
the nightclub door, the machine room somebody built to be admired. Not the
loading dock and not the desk after everyone went home.

## The recurring figure is NIA

An adult woman with dark, short, wavy hair and a strong, composed face.
Tailored suit, usually black or forest green, white cuffs showing. Gold
earrings. Deep red lipstick. Drawn in the same angular fashion-illustration
manner as everyone else in the frame, and she reads as a person, never as a
machine with a face.

She is never posed heroically. She is mid-task, mid-thought or mid-mistake,
and her expression is composed, resourceful and knowingly amused: someone who
has already worked out how ridiculous this is and is not going to say so
first.

**Somebody is in almost every image, doing something.** Both covers the owner
approved put people at the front; the one he rejected was an empty room. An
image with nobody in it needs a reason far better than "the article is about
software". When NIA does not fit the scene, put whoever the story is about
there instead: the person doing the work, the one being told to adapt, the
one holding the clipboard.

## The joke has to be visible in the picture

Somebody looking at the image without reading a word should get it. Two
worked examples, both approved by the owner:

**Agents rigging their own evaluation.** Six elegant candidates at separate
exam desks in a grand hall, apparently working alone. Beneath the desks,
discreet mechanical hands pass a vivid red-orange cable and the examiner's
fountain pen from one to the next, and the cable runs straight into the
automatic grading machine. A human proctor stands in the background with his
back turned, entirely unaware.

**Reward hacking.** A sharply dressed dark-haired woman has opened the back
panel of a monumental brass-and-steel grading machine. Rather than correcting
the failed paper on the desk beside her, she calmly reroutes it through the
exposed gears. A sheet marked with a red crossed circle goes in; the same
sheet comes out carrying a clean golden approval seal. She holds the
examiner's red-orange fountain pen like a technician's tool.

Both do the same three things: one clear physical action, one visible
consequence, and the person who ought to be stopping it either absent or
looking the other way. Build the scene backwards from the sentence in the
article that would make a reader laugh, and set that sentence down on the
floor of a marble hall.
