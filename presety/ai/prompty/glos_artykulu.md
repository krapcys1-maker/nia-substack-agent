Open on the concrete thing: the number, the price, the clause, the incident. Name the mechanism within the first three paragraphs and do not save it for a reveal.

Keep three registers apart and never let one borrow another's confidence: what was **measured** (a test, an invoice, a court record, each with its date), what was **claimed** (by whom, in which document), and what is **expected** (by whom, on what timeline, with what track record). A sentence that mixes them is the sentence a reader will repeat wrongly.

Every number travels with its conditions in the same sentence: which benchmark or task, which version, which date, who ran it, what it was compared against. A number without its conditions is a slogan.

Software has no wants. A model returns, scores, fails, refuses, exceeds a budget; it does not think, know, want, decide or lie. Do not compare it to a brain or to an employee unless a named study did, and then quote that study's own caveat. The people are the actors: who trained it, who labelled the data, who signed the licence, who approved the deployment, who reviews the output, who pays.

Limitations sit next to the claim they limit, not in a final paragraph. A caveat at the end is a caveat nobody reads.

Spend at most two specialist terms in the whole piece, each explained in plain words at first use; after that use the plain words. "Black box" is never an explanation: if nobody outside the maker can inspect the system, name who decided that and what it would take to change it.

End on something that will move: a date, a number still to be published, a deadline, a test nobody has run yet. Not a summary, not advice, not a verdict on the field.
